Words... I guess they mean something

How much do precise definitions or categorizations matter when are talking about different types of technologies? At the very least, these categorizations are necessary to begin organizing inquiries such as the #MoreThanCode report. But do these categories problematically flatten the distinctions between different technologies? At what point are the definitions too vacuous, or on the other hand, too rigid, to be useful? I argue they can be simultaneously vacuous, overly specific, and yet still, somewhat useful. Adjectives and descriptive words are always loaded, and any categorizing principle reliant on definitions, distinctions and typologies are always contested. Yet at the very least, these descriptive terms such as "civic", "public interest", and "social impact" imply we are talking about something with a specific purpose, user, and goal, and not just any ol' technology.

Software are digital programs and applications designed to perform specific tasks. This is the least controversial definition because no value-laden words are attached. However, still, the term “software” often conjures up programs for commercial or individual productivity, whose development, deployment and management are often backed by capital for profit generation.

Civic technology, social impact technology, and public interest technology are subsets of software intended to solve (or ameliorate) problems in the public realm or in the public provisioning of resources and services. The differences between these definitions get much shakier and more contested. McGuinness and Schank in Power to the Public: The Promise of Public Interest Technology distinguishes between some of these terms. First, the difference between civic technology and public interest technology starts with its relationship to technology. The former begins with software or other technology in the service of a problem. The latter, refers more broadly to a way of thinking that begins with “end users” and “iterates in small segments” before reaching scale.

Rooted in the ideals of “open government”, civic technology aims to galvanize change in government decision-making through transparency. Though civic technologies often operate outside or adjacent to governments, its use of the word “civic” implies a relationship to government or governance.

With public interest technology, the substitution from the use of “civic” to “public interest” implies a conception of collective welfare that is greater than simply how people interact with their government. Governments of course care (or theoretically care) about the public interest, and as McGuinness and Schank note, public interest technology can exist inside or outside of governments. However, government is not the only social entity or construct through which human needs or desires are negotiated through.

The term public interest technology derives from “public interest law”. As a former “public interest” lawyer, I am agnostic about the term. It does not meaningfully describe what I did except by who paid me. It was often a term that loosely meant “working for a non-profit” and was used to distinguish from work in a private practice (i.e. law firms) or public service (i.e. government). Are lawyers at the Pacific Legal Foundation, America First Legal, Institute for Justice, etc. working in the public interest? Depends on who you ask. They would think so. I wouldn’t. Regardless of how I feel about it, it counts for example, as “public interest” for the purposes of federal Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Further, organizations embedded in community groups or racial/socio-economic equity movements may (as some respondents in the #MoreThanCode report did) reject the term “public interest” because of their disconnection from professionalized flows of philanthropic capital. But divorced from their connotations of the term, their work is in the opinion of people sympathetic to these issues, certainly “in the public interest”.

We contest the usage of “public interest” because of how words are bound up in socialized identities, connotations, and meanings. But all of this is just to say, we agree that the term means something, even if we disagree on whether it should be named as such.

Lastly, does the term social impact technology refer to something different from public interest technology? As far as I can tell, it doesn’t, though I receive it with different connotations.

“Social impact” does not, on its face, purport to address collective welfare or good. The use of “social” relates to the public, the communal, the collective, but the use of “impact” rather than “good” or another aspirational, welfare-related word focuses on outcomes and “felt” changes. Impacts can be harmful. If we feel that “social impact” means the advancement of the “social good”, it’s because we’ve read that in because it would be silly to promote “socially harmful” impacts. This term is sneaky however, because it substitutes away an equity or welfare framing for something that loosely approximates efficiency, productivity, metrics, indicators, etc. Of course, whether something makes a material difference is the whole point, but to me, what we’re changing ought to get first billing over how much is being changed.
